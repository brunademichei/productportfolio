Work Tax Location User Research

A usability study on a complex, multi-location payroll tax workflow, prototyped end-to-end in Figma Make, that surfaced a clear user need for comparative tax views.

My role
UX Designer & researcher
Method
Usability research (UserTesting)
Prototype
Figma Make (AI-assisted)
Focus
IA, interaction design, research

Overview

Using Figma Make, I created a low-fidelity prototype to support a usability research study run through UserTesting. The whole prototype was built with an AI-assisted (vibe coding) approach, including both the screen designs and the definition of the user tasks used during the study.

The Work Tax Location feature is a complex payroll requirement: employee tax calculations vary depending on their work location. After a discovery session with LPMs, Development Leads, and Software Architects, I led usability research to better understand how users interact with this kind of workflow in practice.

The challenge

The hardest part was translating a highly complex backend payroll component into a simple, intuitive, usable interface. The Work Tax Location logic involves multiple variables that directly impact employee payroll calculations based on location, which makes the information difficult to understand and validate. My role focused on reducing that complexity through clear information architecture and user-centered interaction design, so critical data could be interpreted, compared, and trusted during everyday tasks.

Research approach

I also designed pre-task activities to capture user behavior and expectations before they interacted with the prototype, establishing a clearer baseline for analysis.

The prototype

The screens model an employee's payroll results and the tax detail behind them, with residence and work-location information, jurisdictions, and per-location allocations.

The research report

I synthesized the whole study in a FigJam research report: objectives and success criteria, a participant overview, every participant answer, and the pain points, delights, and suggestions that came out of the sessions. Here are the highlights.

Quick summary & objectives

Quick summary This test evaluates how effectively the Payroll Results Viewer (PRV) handles the complexity of simultaneous multi-state withholding: whether users can understand how income is allocated across work locations and how tax credits are applied to the home state to prevent double taxation. We checked the usability of the PRV for confusion points.
Objectives
  • Identify friction points in the main navigation flow.
  • Assess understanding of critical concepts (taxable amounts, allocations).
  • Observe whether users complete validation tasks without assistance.
  • Check whether multi-level popovers give clear, progressive access to information.
  • Verify the message strip communicates the proration method (Calendar Days).
Success criteria
  • ≥80% success rate on key tasks
  • ≤1 critical error per user
  • Low dependence on support / moderation
  • Qualitative feedback showing clarity in understanding the calculations

Prototype results

Pain points
Multi-level popover goes unexplored. Most users stopped at the top-level number and never dug into the proration details, a false sense of completion.
Period comparison is broken. Users couldn't easily compare Jan vs. Feb because the layout doesn't support a side-by-side view. Some gave up; others were frustrated.
Delights
Layout builds confidence. Average self-rated understanding was high; even users who missed details felt capable. The visual structure is reassuring.
Message strip for proration landed well. The majority noticed it and found it valuable.
Allocation numbers were findable. 5 of 7 located both the NY allocation and the prorated amount, so the IA is discoverable at its core.
Suggestions
Add a visual cue. A "See details" affordance so users know the top-level number is a doorway, not the full answer.
Add a period comparison view. The strongest user desire. Even a simple side-by-side Jan/Feb diff or a "What changed?" summary would resolve most of this.

Takeaways

The prototype performed well overall: users felt confident navigating the screen and responded positively to the proration message strip and the general layout. Most located the key allocation numbers without guidance, so the information architecture is solid at its core. The two clearest gaps are depth and comparison: users stopped at surface-level numbers without realizing more detail lived in the popovers, and they couldn't compare January vs. February because the layout doesn't support a cross-period view. The main opportunities going forward: signal popover depth more explicitly and introduce a period comparison, even a lightweight "what changed" view would address the most recurring need.

What we learned

Users expressed a strong need for comparative views of payroll and tax differences across locations. Seeing those differences side by side would significantly improve clarity, decision-making, and trust in the system.

This finding gave the team a research-backed direction for the feature and validated an approach for presenting complex, location-based tax data in a way users can actually interpret and trust.
